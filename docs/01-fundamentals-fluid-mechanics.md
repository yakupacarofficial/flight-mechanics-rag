# Fundamentals of Fluid Mechanics and Aerodynamics

## Aerodynamic Forces and Moments

The flight trajectory and stability of any aircraft are determined by the dynamic balance of continuous forces and moments acting upon its center of gravity (CG).

*   **Fundamental Forces:** The four primary forces governing flight mechanics are **Lift**, **Drag**, **Thrust**, and **Weight**. Lift acts perpendicular to the relative wind to overcome Weight. Thrust is generated by the propulsion system to overcome Drag, which acts parallel to the relative wind. In steady, unaccelerated flight, Lift equals Weight, and Thrust equals Drag.
*   **Aerodynamic Moments:** As aerodynamic forces are distributed across the airframe, they generate rotational tendencies around the aircraft's three spatial axes. 
    *   **Pitch:** Rotation around the lateral axis, nose up or down, primarily controlled by the elevator.
    *   **Roll:** Rotation around the longitudinal axis, banking left or right, controlled by ailerons.
    *   **Yaw:** Rotation around the vertical axis, moving the nose left or right, controlled by the rudder. 
    Accurate calculation of these moment coefficients is critical for defining the flight envelope and tuning autonomous mission tracking algorithms.

## Boundary Layer and Flow Regimes

Understanding how air interacts with the surface of an aircraft is crucial for predicting aerodynamic drag and stall characteristics.

*   **Laminar and Turbulent Flow:** In **Laminar Flow**, air molecules move smoothly in parallel layers with minimal mixing, resulting in lower skin friction but high susceptibility to separation. In **Turbulent Flow**, the streamlines break up into chaotic, mixing eddies. While turbulent flow increases skin friction drag, its higher kinetic energy delays flow separation at higher angles of attack.
*   **Boundary Layer and Separation:** The **Boundary Layer** is the thin sheet of air directly in contact with the airfoil surface where viscous forces dominate, and velocity drops from the freestream value to zero at the surface (no-slip condition). **Flow Separation** occurs when the boundary layer loses its kinetic energy against an adverse pressure gradient, detaching from the surface. This separation creates a massive turbulent wake, causing a sudden loss of lift and a spike in drag, a condition known as a stall.

## Bernoulli's Principle and Fluid Dynamics

**Bernoulli's Principle** is a fundamental expression of the conservation of energy in a moving fluid. It dictates that for an inviscid, incompressible fluid in steady flow, an increase in the fluid's velocity occurs simultaneously with a decrease in its static pressure.

Mathematically, the sum of static pressure and dynamic pressure remains constant along a streamline. When air accelerates over the curved upper surface of an airfoil, its static pressure drops significantly compared to the slower-moving air beneath the wing. This pressure differential across the upper and lower surfaces is the primary physical mechanism that generates aerodynamic lift.

## Pitot Tube and Airspeed Measurement

The principles of fluid dynamics are practically applied in aircraft instrumentation through the **Pitot-Static System**, which is essential for determining airspeed.

*   **Pressure Components:** The system measures two distinct pressures. **Static Pressure** is the ambient atmospheric pressure of the undisturbed air. **Stagnation Pressure** (or Total Pressure) is the pressure measured at a point where the airflow is brought to a complete stop (stagnation point), capturing both the static pressure and the kinetic energy of the moving air.
*   **Airspeed Calculation:** A **Pitot Tube** faces directly into the relative wind to capture stagnation pressure, while static ports measure the ambient pressure. By applying Bernoulli's equation, the instrument subtracts static pressure from stagnation pressure to isolate the **Dynamic Pressure**. This dynamic pressure is directly proportional to the square of the aircraft's velocity, allowing the system to output an accurate airspeed reading.

## Dimensionless Numbers: Mach and Reynolds

Dimensionless parameters are critical for aerodynamic scaling, allowing engineers to correlate data between wind tunnel models, software simulations (like Gazebo or X-Plane), and real-world flight testing.

*   **Reynolds Number (Re):** This number represents the ratio of inertial forces to viscous forces within a fluid. It predicts flow patterns in different fluid flow situations. Unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) typically operate in low Reynolds number regimes compared to commercial jets, meaning viscous forces play a much larger role. This low-Re environment makes boundary layers thicker and more prone to early separation, drastically altering aerodynamic coefficients.
*   **Mach Number (M):** This is the ratio of the aircraft's true airspeed to the local speed of sound. It defines the compressibility of the flow. Below Mach 0.3, air density remains relatively constant, and the flow is treated as **incompressible**. Above this threshold, density changes must be factored into aerodynamic calculations. Most small-scale drones and UAVs operate well within the incompressible subsonic regime.
